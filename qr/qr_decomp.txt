================
QR Decomposition
================

Basic Understanding
===================

What is QR decomposition?
-------------------------

Any matrix :math:`A` can be decomposed as a product of an *orthogonal matrix*
:math:`Q` and an upper triangular matrix :math:`R`.

.. math::

   A = Q R


and what is an orthogonal matrix?
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

:math:`Q` is *orthogonal* if it preserves dot products after vector
transformation.  That is,

.. math::

   Q \mathbf{u} \cdot Q \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}

or using transpose operations,

.. math::

   \mathbf{u}^T Q^T Q \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{v}.

   \Rightarrow \mathbf{u}^T \left(Q^T Q - I\right) \mathbf{v} = 0

Since :math:`\mathbf{u}` and :math:`\mathbf{v}` are arbitrary, :math:`Q^T Q =
I` and we get the orthogonality property,

.. math::

   Q^{-1} = Q^T.

The most common example of an orthogonal matrix is the rotation matrix,

.. math::

   R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}
      \cos \theta & \sin \theta \\
      -\sin \theta & \cos \theta
   \end{pmatrix}

which rotates a vector by angle :math:`\theta`.  (Almost universally written as
:math:`R`; no relation to the upper triangular matrix above.)

Another is the reflection matrix,

.. math::

   r(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}
      \cos \theta & \sin \theta \\
      \sin \theta & -\cos \theta
   \end{pmatrix}

which reflects a vector along a line of angle :math:`\theta / 2`.  Both are
important tools for construction of :math:`Q`.

The most interesting property of the orthogonal matrix is the *orthogonality*
of its column vectors.  If :math:`Q = ( \mathbf{q}_1, \mathbf{q}_2,
\mathbf{q}_3, \ldots )`, then

.. math::

   Q^T Q &=
      \begin{pmatrix}
         \mathbf{q}_1 \\ \mathbf{q}_2 \\ \mathbf{q}_3 \\ \vdots
      \end{pmatrix}
      \begin{pmatrix}
         \mathbf{q}_1 & \mathbf{q}_2 & \mathbf{q}_3 & \cdots
      \end{pmatrix}

   &=
      \begin{pmatrix}
         \mathbf{q}_1^T \mathbf{q}_1
         & \mathbf{q}_1^T \mathbf{q}_2
         & \mathbf{q}_1^T \mathbf{q}_3
         & \cdots \\[0.5em]
         \mathbf{q}_2^T \mathbf{q}_1
         & \mathbf{q}_2^T \mathbf{q}_2
         & \mathbf{q}_2^T \mathbf{q}_3
         & \cdots \\[0.5em]
         \mathbf{q}_3^T \mathbf{q}_1
         & \mathbf{q}_3^T \mathbf{q}_2
         & \mathbf{q}_3^T \mathbf{q}_3
         & \cdots \\[0.5em]
         \vdots & & & \ddots
      \end{pmatrix}

Since :math:`Q^T Q = I`, it must be that :math:`\mathbf{q}_i^T \mathbf{q}_j =
\delta_{ij}`.  The column vectors of :math:`Q` form an orthogonal basis.


Why would you do this?
----------------------

If you know :math:`Q` and `R`, then you can solve systems of equations without
calculating an inverse operator.  If you are trying to solve :math:`\mathbf{x}`
in :math:`A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}`, then

.. math::

   A \mathbf{x} = Q R \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}

   \Rightarrow R \mathbf{x} = Q^{-1} \mathbf{b} = Q^T \mathbf{b}

Since :math:`R` is upper triangular, use *back substitution* to compute
:math:`\mathbf{x}` from bottom to top.  (:math:`R^{-1}` is also an upper
tridiagonal matrix.)

.. * Solving an overdetermined system based on minimizing :math:`|Ax - b|`.

.. * Computing the eigenvalues of :math:`A` ("QR algorithm")

.. * :math:`Q` provides a way to change the basis of :math:`\mathbf{x}` (although
   I don't know how useful this is unless you have some control over :math:`Q`)


What is the shape of Q and R if the input matrix is m×n with m≥n?
-----------------------------------------------------------------

For a generic matrix :math:`A_{m,n}`, the decomposition is

.. math::

   A_{m,n} = Q_{m,m} \times R_{m,n}.

The number of orthonormal column vectors :math:`\mathbf{q}` matches the number
of rows of :math:`A`.  The orthogonal space defined by :math:`Q` may be either
larger or smaller than the solution(s) to A.

The deterministic case (:math:`m = n`) is relatively straightforward.
:math:`R` is exactly tridiagonal.

*Overdetermined* systems (:math:`m > n`) have more equations than variables,
and have no solutions.  The canonical example is a least-squares fit, where one
instead minimizes :math:`\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2`.

* The bottom rows of :math:`R` are filled with zeros.  We still describe
  :math:`R` as upper triangular.  When we try to solve

  .. math::

     R \mathbf{x} = Q^T \mathbf{b},

  the lower :math:`r` are :math:`q_{rk} b_k = 0`, which is in most cases a
  contradiction.

* The :math:`m` column vectors of :math:`Q` describe a space that is larger
  than :math:`\mathbf{x}`.

*Underdetermined* systems (:math:`m < n`) are "wide" and do not have unique
solutions.  When these occur, one may seek some norm-based minimum
:math:`\|\mathbf{x}\|` to the problem.

There are inherent issues with an undetermined QR decomposition.

* :math:`R` is *upper trapezoidal*, with the final row having multiple values.
  Back substitution is not possible, and the matrix is not meaningfully
  invertible.

* The columns of :math:`Q` only span :math:`\mathbb{R}^m`, which is smaller
  than the space of :math:`\mathbf{x}`.

In other words, the QR decomposition does not help to solve :math:`A \mathbf{x}
= \mathbf{b}`.

One approach to undetermined systems is to instead decompose :math:`A^T`, which
can be solved by QR-decomposition, and whose solution can solve :math:`\min
\|\mathbf{x}\|`.  (But this is another topic...)


How does QR decomposition differ from LU decomposition?
-------------------------------------------------------

A lot of this needs more context than has been written up yet.  But...

* QR is more expensive:

  * LU is :math:`\frac{2}{3} n^3`

  * QR is (typically) :math:`2 m n^2 - \frac{2}{3} n^3`, or :math:`\frac{4}{3}
    n^3`.

  For square matrics, QR is about 2x the cost of LU.

* QR is generally more *stable*

  * If :math:`A` is rank-deficit, it is handled naturally in QR: you end up
    with a row of zeros in :math:`R`.

    LU needs some method to detect and pivot around it.

  * :math:`Q` has a condition number of 1, so it's never a problem.

    Good luck with LU!

* QR works in *nondeterminate systems*

  * Overdetermined least-squares data fitting

  * Underdetermined solutions to :math:`\min \| A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b} \|`
    (by decomposition of :math:`A^T`).

Summary:

   QR is more expensive, but it is also more stable and overall useful over a
   wider number of applications.


Can all matrices be decomposed using QR?
----------------------------------------

Yes, although this is more a procedural fact.

* One can generate an orthonormal vector set from any set of column vectors.
  In this case, it is the column vectors of :math:`A`.

* QR-decomposition is an iterative change-of-basis which work over iteratively
  smaller segments of :math:`A`, eventually producing :math:`R`.

But this is just describing the various algorithms for decomposition.
